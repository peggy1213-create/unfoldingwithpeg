Every few years, L&D rediscovers the same trap with a new technology. This year it is AI coaching.

The headline numbers are impressive. ACCA's magazine cites an estimate of about 23 million active users on AI coaching platforms, plus a Conference Board study in which 89% of users said a session gave them specific, useful next steps and 91% said they would use it again ([Could your next coach be a bot?](https://abmagazine.accaglobal.com/content/abmagazine/global/articles/2026/aug/careers/could-your-next-coach-be-a-bot-.html)). Valence reports more than a million coaching conversations across Fortune 500 clients ([People Managing People](https://peoplemanagingpeople.com/performance-management/ai-coaching-results/)).

When I read numbers like these, my learning-designer reflex kicks in. These are smile sheets.

**What Kirkpatrick would ask**\
Anyone who has evaluated a training program knows the Kirkpatrick model. Level 1 is reaction, did people like it. Level 2 is learning, did they gain knowledge or skill. Level 3 is behavior, do they do anything differently at work. Level 4 is results, did it matter for the organization.

AI coaching is repeating the pattern at scale. "Would you use it again" is a level 1 question. Session counts, daily active users and nudge open rates are not even level 1. They measure attendance.

**The trial that should worry buyers**\
David Rice at People Managing People lays out the evidence carefully in [Your AI Coach Has Great Reviews and No Results](https://peoplemanagingpeople.com/performance-management/ai-coaching-results/). In 2022, a randomized trial led by Nicky Terblanche found that an AI chatbot helped people reach their goals about as well as human coaches did. That study became the founding evidence for the whole category. In 2026, Terblanche co-authored a second trial with 114 coachees inside one global organization, comparing accredited human coaches with an AI coach on validated measures of goals, motivation, resilience and wellbeing. Human coaching improved every outcome. The AI arm did not beat the no-coaching control group on a single primary measure.

Same researcher, opposite headline. Rice's reading is that the 2022 AI had a narrow job, walking someone through a structured goal protocol, while the 2026 AI was asked to be the coach. The researchers point to co-regulation, the small mutual adjustments two people make in response to each other, as what carried the human effect.

Two caveats matter. It was one organization and one chatbot, and newer agentic tools with memory were not tested. Still, the burden of proof has shifted. A tool that reports only engagement is, in Rice's framing, selling satisfaction and calling it development.

**Where coaching actually leaks**\
The more useful question is not human versus AI. It is where behavior change breaks down, and what each can do about it.

BTS, a firm that sells both coaching and AI (read it with that in mind), names three gaps in traditional coaching in [How AI solves coaching's three critical gaps](https://bts.com/whitepapers/how-ai-solves-coachings-three-critical-gaps). Coaching runs on the coachee's own account of themselves, and self-report is biased. The paper cites a Leadership IQ study in which 58% of C-suite leaders overestimate their own performance. The real evidence of change is scattered across hundreds of small moments at work that no coach ever sees. And sessions held every two to four weeks leave long gaps in which fresh intentions fade.

Read that list as a learning designer and it is a transfer problem. The session is not where learning happens. It is where intention is formed. Transfer happens on a Tuesday afternoon, in a delegation message or a tense one-on-one. BTS argues that AI is most valuable not when it imitates the coach but when it makes that Tuesday visible.

**Sparring partner and sensei**\
Nadav Klein at INSEAD offers a metaphor I keep coming back to in [AI and the Future of Executive Coaching](https://knowledge.insead.edu/career-development/ai-and-future-executive-coaching). AI is the sparring partner, endlessly patient, always available, happy to run the same drill again. The human coach is the sensei, who sees the whole person and whose challenge lands because of the relationship behind it. Letting down a trusted person, Klein notes, carries a weight that ignoring an app never will.

Rehearsal, reflection, preparation and keeping commitments alive between sessions are bounded tasks, and that is where AI has shown value. Accountability and meaning-making live in relationships.

**Designing for level 3**\
If I were scoping an AI coaching program, or building one into a product, this is what I would change.

1. **Write the behavior first**: before choosing a tool, name the two or three behaviors you expect to see more of, in observable terms. "Gives feedback within 48 hours" is designable. "Becomes a better leader" is not.
2. **Measure where work happens**: replace "would you use it again" with signals from other people and from the work itself, such as a short pulse from direct reports, or whether a committed action actually showed up.
3. **Watch self-initiated use**: a practitioner quoted in Rice's piece suggests the best early signal is whether people open the tool without being told to. Assigned use proves compliance. Coming back on your own suggests it helped.
4. **Give AI the reps, give humans the accountability**: let AI handle practice and between-session follow-through. Keep a person, a coach, a manager or a peer, for the moments when someone needs to be held to a promise.
5. **Decide deliberately who gets a human**: the 2026 trial found that starting self-efficacy and hope predicted who benefited most. The people with the least confidence are the ones an unsupervised bot serves worst.

That last point connects to something I wrote two weeks ago, [Being Ambitious with AI Is a Learning Problem](https://studiounfolding.cc/en/posts/ambition-with-ai-is-a-learning-problem/), where I argued that AI ambition is really a self-efficacy problem. If confidence predicts who gains from coaching, then handing a chatbot to the people who most need support and calling it democratization quietly widens the gap it claims to close.

**The peer layer in between**\
There is one more option the debate tends to skip. Between an expensive human coach and an always-on bot sits the peer. In the community learning work I do, the most reliable source of follow-through is not a reminder. It is someone who will ask you next week, "so, did you try it?" Peers are not senseis, but they bring a small, real social stake, exactly what Klein says AI lacks. Pair the bot's patience with a peer's question and you get something much closer to level 3 than either can reach alone.

AI coaching can genuinely widen access to something that used to be reserved for executives, and I want that to happen. But access to a pleasant conversation is not the same as access to growth.
