I have a soft spot for manifestation, and an equally strong allergy to how it is usually sold. Write it down, see it clearly, believe it, and the universe rearranges itself. Having worked in learning and development for many years, I keep wanting to ask a less mystical question. What actually changes in a person between the moment they set an intention and the moment something happens?

Marisa Edmonds, a self-described sceptic, asks the same thing in [The Science of Manifestation, is there really evidence to support it?](https://www.womeninneuroscienceuk.org/post/the-science-of-manifestation-is-there-really-evidence-to-support-it) for Women in Neuroscience UK. Her answer sits, in her words, somewhere in the middle. Manifestation is not magic, but it is not nothing either. Read it closely and it describes something I recognise from my own work with adult learners, a structured way of changing attention, state and behaviour.

**What the science actually supports**\
Edmonds walks through a handful of mechanisms.

- **Attention**: the brain filters what it notices, and what you rehearse becomes salient. The "coincidence" of seeing opportunities everywhere after setting a goal is mostly your filter changing, not the world.
- **State**: under stress, the body narrows attention to immediate threats and long-term planning gets pushed aside. A calmer state gives you back the cognitive space to think ahead.
- **Rehearsal**: imagining an action recruits some of the same systems used to plan and perform it, which is why athletes practise in their heads.
- **Self-efficacy**: belief that you can do something affects how hard you try and how long you keep going.

None of this requires the universe to cooperate. It requires you to notice, to settle, to practise and to persist. Edmonds lands on the same conclusion. The mechanism is your attention, your choices and your repeated actions.

She is also honest about the limits. Manifestation will not override structural barriers, timing or luck, and many of the claims circulating online lean on podcasts and popular books rather than studies. I appreciate that, because the most useful version of manifestation is the one that knows what it cannot do.

**The step that backfires**\
Here is the part that the vision board culture rarely mentions. The signature move of manifestation, vividly imagining the dream as if it has already happened, has been tested directly, and it does not always help.

Psychologists Heather Barry Kappes and Gabriele Oettingen ran a series of experiments on positive fantasies, summarised by the British Psychological Society in [Why positive fantasies make your dreams less likely to come true](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/why-positive-fantasies-make-your-dreams-less-likely-come-true). People who indulged in an idealised picture of the future showed lower energy, measured in one study by a drop in blood pressure. In another, people who fantasised positively about their coming week reported feeling less energised, and a week later had achieved fewer of their goals than people who simply daydreamed freely.

The researchers' explanation is uncomfortable and convincing. A vivid fantasy lets you taste the reward in advance. Your body relaxes as if the work were done. The feeling of arrival arrives early, and the energy to actually get there quietly leaves.

This does not mean dreaming is bad. It means dreaming is only half of the exercise.

**Put the obstacle back in the picture**\
Oettingen's answer is a practice called mental contrasting, popularised as WOOP, Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan. You still picture the outcome you want. Then you deliberately picture what inside you is most likely to get in the way. Not the economy or your boss, but your own habit, fear or excuse. Then you make a plan for that moment.

The plan has a specific shape, an if-then sentence. If I feel like skipping my morning practice because I slept badly, then I will do five minutes instead of thirty. Peter Gollwitzer's research on these implementation intentions, which Edmonds also cites, consistently finds that linking a situation to a response makes people far more likely to follow through than holding a goal alone.

Anyone who works in learning and development will recognise this as the difference between an inspiring workshop and one that changes behaviour. Inspiration describes the destination. Transfer happens when people rehearse the exact moment they will be tempted to fall back into the old pattern, and decide in advance what they will do instead.

**A manifestation practice I would actually design**\
If I were turning manifestation into a learning program, or just into a Sunday evening habit, it would look less like a vision board and more like this.

1. **Name the wish small enough to act on**: not "be confident at work", but "speak once in every team meeting this month".
2. **Picture the outcome, briefly**: enjoy it for a minute. It matters, because it tells you why the effort is worth it.
3. **Picture your obstacle, honestly**: what usually happens inside you right before you stay quiet? Be specific.
4. **Write one if-then plan**: if I notice I am rehearsing my sentence for too long, then I will say the first half out loud.
5. **Start smaller than feels necessary**: Sharon Lechter's [advice on habits](https://sharonlechter.com/blog/how-habits-shape-behavior-and-influence-long-term-outcomes/) is to wake up ten minutes earlier, not two hours, and to treat setbacks as data rather than verdicts.
6. **Reflect and share**: the Haus of Coaching [growth mindset blueprint](https://northants-chamber.co.uk/latest-news/your-2026-growth-mindset-a-leaders-blueprint-for-meaningful-progress/) suggests ending the day with two questions, what worked and what did I learn, and telling a peer or mentor what you are working on.

Notice what this keeps from manifestation. It keeps the clear intention, the regular rehearsal, the calmer state that comes from having a plan, and the belief that grows each time you follow through. What it removes is the promise that wanting is enough.

**Believing, the useful way**\
I think this is why manifestation resonates with so many people, and why it disappoints so many too. The belief part is real. Self-efficacy does change how long we persist. But belief is built, not declared. It grows from small, repeated experiences of doing the thing, especially on the days when the obstacle shows up exactly as you predicted and you handle it anyway.

So by all means, picture the life you want. Then picture the moment you are most likely to talk yourself out of it, and decide now what you will do when it comes. That second picture is less pleasant. It is also the one that does the work.
